# Author interview and writing decisions

## Purpose and activation

Establish what the author wants to say before planning how to say it. Use for a new work, an unclear contribution, or a substantive change of argument, audience or destination. Default to the interview; the author can say “skip the grilling”. Do not restart settled questions during an authorised section draft or narrow edit.

This procedure is self-contained. Follow the interview and documentation instructions below without loading another interviewing or domain-modelling skill. Model the subject being explained: its terms, distinctions, relationships and claims. Use the document's established editorial workspace.

## Read before asking

- Open the current author text, comments, deletions, commission, available source materials and previous editorial decisions.
- Save a short source inventory: what was read, what is established, conflicting accounts, missing materials and questions only the author can answer.
- Research retrievable facts through the permitted source routes. Ask the author about intended meaning, interpretation, priorities and choices.
- Distinguish author statements, evidence, model inferences and proposals. An archive's contents or chronology do not determine the paper's argument.

## Interview one decision at a time

Choose the next question from the unresolved dependencies; do not send a questionnaire or ask for information already supplied. Give the immediate context, a proposed interpretation or recommendation grounded in the materials, and the consequence of the choice. Wait for the answer before asking the next question. Do not lead the author into accepting the model's preferred thesis.

Cover applicable branches; record already answered or irrelevant branches without asking them again:

- **Argument and lessons:** What should readers conclude? What does the author believe the work shows? Which findings, contributions or practical lessons lead, and why? What would misrepresent the point?
- **Reader and use:** Who is reading, why, what do they know, what must be explained, and what is irrelevant?
- **Domain:** Which terms and distinctions carry the argument? What examples clarify them? Which processes, actors or kinds of evidence must remain distinct?
- **Genre and destination:** Paper, report, essay or other form; intended venue and required sections; relevant papers as models for explanation or argument. Inspect supplied examples before inferring their conventions.
- **Evidence and disagreement:** What supports each proposed claim? What negative findings, rival explanations or objections matter? Which interpretation is the author's, and which questions remain evidentially unresolved?
- **Emphasis and boundaries:** What must appear early, what can move to an appendix or reusable resource, what is private, and which exact wording or deletions are protected?

When a term conflicts with the current glossary or the author's earlier usage, quote the two meanings and ask which is intended. Propose precise terms for overloaded expressions. Test important distinctions and relationships with a concrete example or edge case; use the author's materials where possible and label invented scenarios. Compare descriptions of the work with the source records and surface specific discrepancies. Save resolved terms and relationships as they are agreed, alongside the writing decisions; do not postpone recording them until the interview ends.

Author intent does not verify an empirical claim. Record a source conflict separately with its locator; ask about the intended argument without silently weakening it or presenting unsupported content as established.

## Save answers and decisions as they arise

Use existing interview, declaration and decision records. Create a canonical `record-author-interview.md` or `record-writing-decisions.md` only when the existing workspace has no equivalent. Dates, versions and update history belong in metadata; named snapshots can live in archive/offcuts. Use headings and bullets so the author and receiving agents can inspect the records.

For each substantive exchange, retain:

- The question, exact available answer and source locator. Label a paraphrase; preserve protected wording verbatim.
- The interpretation proposed by the model and the author's actual response. Never manufacture a quotation or retrospective confirmation.
- The decision and reason, meaningful alternatives discussed, scope, status and unresolved issue.
- The declarations, claim IDs, sections, visuals or resources affected, and who needs the decision next.

Use lightweight **writing decision records**, equivalent in function to ADRs: context/question, decision, reasons, alternatives, consequences and status. Small decisions can share one record with stable IDs and headings. Keep the subject glossary separate from the argument decisions; create or update the project's existing `CONTEXT.md` only if that is its established domain-glossary convention. Do not impose engineering ADR criteria that would omit an important authorial choice.

## Confirm direction before outlining

Show a short synthesis in the author's terms: central argument, ranked lessons/contributions, reader, destination, necessary domain distinctions and remaining questions. Ask whether it captures the intended direction. Existing explicit confirmation counts; do not request it again.

Before proposing the substantive outline, record shared understanding or the author's explicit skip/provisional-plan instruction. A skip waives the interview, not author fidelity, evidence requirements or later review points. Identify unsettled assumptions in the editorial record; do not present them as decisions. If an authorial choice is required and unanswered, keep it pending and continue only work independent of that choice.

## Keep decisions current during writing

Capture subsequent feedback in the same record. A correction can update a decision directly; do not force another interview when the author has already stated the answer. Preserve the earlier state as superseded, with the reason and source of the change. Ask one focused follow-up only when the new direction remains ambiguous.

Update the affected purpose/reader declaration, proposition register, narrative plan and section briefs. Name the drafts and reviews that need reopening. Do not rewrite unrelated sections or restore deleted material. Writers and independent critics must read the current decisions and exact author passages before acting; record the versions actually loaded. Include interview records, decisions and their downstream links in any requested editorial inspector.
