# Instructional Visual and Video Principles

Source basis: Dominik Lukeš's *Instructional Videos Framework*, including its six design tasks, critical use of Mayer's multimedia-learning principles and practical production guidance. This reference extends that synthesis across media. Research principles, Dominik's contextual judgements and newer design patterns have different evidence status; see [sources and limits](sources-multimedia-learning.md).

## Six Design Tasks

### 1. Direct attention

- Make the relevant element and explanatory structure perceptible. Use position, headings, contrast, pointers or highlighting with a specific referent.
- Place labels near their referents. Name relationships when a line, arrow, grouping or proximity could otherwise mean several things: causes, precedes, contains, measures and is associated with are different claims.
- Reveal a step when sequence matters; retain a stable whole or comparison when learners need to inspect relationships together.
- Use cursor movement, crop, zoom or animation as signals. Avoid multiple competing focal points. Colour alone should not carry the distinction.

### 2. Minimise distraction and support processing

- Reduce extraneous cognitive load: remove material and interface work that competes with the learning task. Do not remove necessary complexity merely to make a visual look clean.
- Simplify, enlarge or crop around the current explanation while preserving the context needed to interpret it. Keep relevant scales, qualifiers, reference states and comparisons.
- In narrated material, avoid making learners read dense duplicate prose while following a different visual. Short labels, equations, unfamiliar spellings and deliberate quotations can still be useful.
- In silent graphics, self-paced slides and interactive views, text may carry the explanation. Do not apply narration-specific redundancy or modality advice as a ban on text.
- Accessibility captions and transcripts are access routes, not decorative duplicate text. Plan their placement so they do not obscure the visual referent.

### 3. Support conceptualisation and integration

- Define unfamiliar components and terms before relying on their complex interactions: pretraining should remove a prerequisite obstacle, not impose a glossary on experts.
- Connect new material to prior knowledge, a relatable example and a visible result. Use graphics to explain relationships, structure, motion or process.
- Provide coherent units with explicit connections. Separating every concept can hide the relationship learners are meant to understand.
- An overview before detail can establish a map for a complex explanation. A concrete worked example or an expert's direct lookup may be a better opening.
- Use a synthesis, prediction, explanation, comparison or practice task when it helps learners integrate the material. Do not add a quiz to every graphic.
- For linked evidence, an internal summary can explain a source's claim, relevance and limits before a separate external link. Preserve direct source access when lookup or verification is the task; do not gate it behind mandatory reading.

### 4. Make a personal connection

- Address the learner directly where appropriate and make the material's relevance clear through its task and examples.
- Prefer natural, clear explanation over compressed promotional delivery. Use a clear outline; script tightly where precision requires it.
- A talking head is optional. Voice, relevance and direct address can establish connection; a face can also compete with a dense visual.
- Static materials need neither an invented speaker nor synthetic narration to count as instruction.

### 5. Enable navigation and learner control

- Make it easy to find the gist, revisit a detail and compare related material. Chunk by meaning; avoid fragmenting a connected explanation into arbitrary micro-units.
- Match control to the medium: reading order and inspectable detail in a graphic; browsing and an accessible export for slides; meaningful state controls in an interactive view; pause, seek, speed and chapters for video where supported.
- For interactive material, make actions and resulting states understandable; provide reset or a route back when exploration changes context. Essential content must not depend on hover alone.
- Plan readable type, contrast, non-colour cues, text descriptions or structured data, keyboard operation and visible focus as relevant. Test access in the actual output.
- Provide captions, a transcript and descriptions of essential visual information for relevant audio/video content. Consider reduced-motion and static/step-through alternatives for animated interfaces.
- Avoid preroll and unnecessary barriers to the content.

### 6. Balance competing needs

- Adapt detail, pace, terminology, examples and control to prior knowledge and the viewing situation. Name uncertainty about the audience rather than silently assuming expertise.
- Give beginners support without preventing experienced learners from scanning or skipping it. Learner control alone does not provide novice guidance.
- Make visually compelling work serve understanding: use scale to show relative size, contrast to identify the current referent and composition to expose relationships. Decorative novelty is not a learning outcome.
- Preserve evidence fidelity. Distinguish observed data, source claims, hypothetical examples, model outputs and interpretation when a learner could mistake one for another. Do not imply causality through arrows or motion when the evidence shows association.

## Multimedia-Learning Checks and Their Scope

Use these as research-informed questions, not guaranteed effects or universal production rules. They summarise the framework's selection from Mayer; they are not an exhaustive or immutable taxonomy.

- **Multimedia:** do words and relevant visuals each contribute to understanding? A simple textual answer need not acquire an illustration.
- **Coherence:** does removing extraneous material leave the explanation intact?
- **Signalling:** can the learner find the structure and current focus?
- **Spatial contiguity:** are corresponding words and graphics close enough to integrate without unnecessary searching?
- **Temporal contiguity:** in a changing display, is the corresponding explanation available while the event is visible? Static pages do not need artificial timing.
- **Segmenting:** can a learner process coherent portions at a useful pace without losing their connections?
- **Pretraining:** are needed component names and characteristics available before a difficult interaction?
- **Redundancy and modality:** for the actual task, does speech support the visual or compete with reading? Do not generalise a narrated-graphics finding into removal of captions or essential standalone text.
- **Personalisation and voice:** is the explanation direct and intelligible? Do not infer a universal ban on synthetic voices or a requirement for a presenter image from older comparisons.
- **Generative activity:** does a prompt engage explanation, prediction, comparison or application rather than add an unrelated interaction?

## What Animation Is For

Animation is a cognitive-load and attention decision, not a universal good or bad.

For beginners learning important, unfamiliar and conceptually dense material — for example in physics or software engineering — stable inspection often supports processing. Remove movement that competes with the explanation. Let the learner inspect a diagram, equation, interface state or code example long enough to integrate it with the words.

For orientation, general instruction and material with lower processing demands, modest motion can help sustain attention and make the spoken progression perceptible. This may include a short key phrase changing as the speaker reaches a new idea, a restrained transition between examples, or a crop or highlight. Such motion need not depict a physical process; keep it subordinate to the explanation. This is contextual design guidance, not a claim that an animated overview always teaches better.

Animation can direct attention, show manipulation, reveal sequence or causal change, or make an invisible process visible. Check that the depicted relationship is supported. A moving arrow is not evidence of causation.

Avoid both continuous decorative motion and a blanket motion ban. Calibrate frequency and speed separately. Preserve key states with holds, replay, stepping or a static comparison when details would otherwise disappear. Reduced motion should retain the explanation's meaning, not merely remove the only representation of change.

## Duration and Mode Choice

There is no ideal fixed length. Duration follows subject complexity, prior knowledge and navigation. A one-minute video may suit one action; ten- to twenty-minute sections may be reasonable when warranted. Do not compress an explanation to imitate an advert or pad it to meet a nominal lesson length.

Use video when time, motion, speech or guided attention adds value. Use stable graphics for inspection, spatial relationships and comparison; interactive views when manipulating or selecting changes what learners can understand. Add text or stills when learners need to skim, scan, quote, search or revisit exact details. A transcript is a learning resource as well as an accessibility route.
