# Worked Examples

These are illustrative designs, not findings from learner trials. Adapt them to the actual sources and audience.

## One Relationship, Four Media: A Filling Tank

Outcome: explain why the water level rises when inflow exceeds outflow. Audience: beginners who know litres and minutes. Given teaching model: tank starts with 20 litres; fixed inflow 6 litres/minute and outflow 4 litres/minute; no overflow during the interval. The derived net gain is 2 litres/minute, so after 5 minutes the tank contains 30 litres. The fixed rates and absence of overflow are assumptions, not measured observations.

### Presenter-led slides

1. Show the tank and define inflow, outflow and volume with adjacent labels.
2. Show both rates together; highlight inflow and outflow in turn as the speaker names them. Keep their values visible while deriving `6 − 4 = 2 litres/minute`.
3. Hold a before/after comparison, `20 litres` and `30 litres after 5 minutes`, beside the explanation. Ask what would happen if both rates were equal.

The point is the relationship between rates, not three disconnected vocabulary slides. An independent reading export includes the net-gain explanation; speaker cues alone are insufficient. Still holds support inspection; no continuously animated water is needed.

### Static explanatory graphic

Use one labelled tank with an inward arrow, `inflow: 6 litres/minute`, and an outward arrow, `outflow: 4 litres/minute`. Put `net gain: 2 litres/minute` beside the water level. Add two clearly ordered snapshots at 0 and 5 minutes, with volume labels and the model assumptions nearby.

Text equivalent: the tank gains two litres each minute because six enter and four leave; it increases from twenty to thirty litres in five minutes under the stated assumptions. The arrows encode flow, not evidence from a real experiment. No timed reading or animation is implied.

### Interactive view

Begin with the same fixed-rate model. Let the learner change inflow while outflow stays fixed, predict the direction of change, then step forward one minute. Keep rates, elapsed time and volume visible; provide reset to the initial state and a stable numeric comparison. Explain the result beside the tank.

Limit this example to states where the model applies, or define and explain empty/full boundary behaviour before adding those states. Do not silently produce negative volume. A keyboard-accessible numeric input and step button can serve the same purpose as a slider. A text result makes the relationship available without watching motion.

### Narrated video

Show and name the components first. Highlight the two rates as they are spoken, then show the level rising while the net gain is explained. Stop at a labelled before/after state so learners can inspect the arithmetic. Provide captions and a transcript that includes the essential visual change.

Motion shows accumulation; an attention cue can also draw the eye to the rate currently discussed. Either may help without requiring motion throughout. Timing follows the explanation. For a short single-concept clip, a separate chapter menu may add no value.

Review all four: can the learner predict the result for equal rates and explain why? A correct answer on this task would be evidence about that learner and task, not proof of universal design superiority.

## Research Comparison: Explain Before Opening a Source

Request: help unfamiliar readers compare two papers. Supplied facts: A reports an observational association; B uses random assignment. No results or population details have been supplied.

Design: use aligned comparison cards for study design, measured outcome, result and limits. Leave unsupported fields pending until the papers are read. Define observational and randomised designs before relying on the distinction. Do not infer that the studies answer the same question or that B's particular conclusion is automatically valid.

If a graph adds value, label links with their actual relation, such as `reports an association between` or `randomly assigns participants to`. Do not draw a causal arrow for A merely to simplify the layout.

Selecting a card reveals a local explanation: what the source reports, how it bears on the comparison, and what is the author's interpretation. Offer a clearly labelled `Read paper` link separately, keeping the comparison state on return. This pattern supports a learning task; an expert asking only to retrieve a paper should get the direct link without a compulsory overview or summary panel.

Review: can a reader state each study's evidential role without opening both papers, and still reach the originals for verification? Can the same information and links be reached by keyboard?

## Motion Counterexample: Orientation and Dense Detail

A general orientation video may use a restrained highlight and changing key phrase to track the speaker across three familiar examples. The movement guides attention even though it does not model a physical process.

A novice explanation of an unfamiliar equation should usually hold the equation and symbol definitions still while the terms are related. A step reveal may help, followed by a stable complete expression. The decision depends on processing demand, not a universal preference for animation or stillness.
